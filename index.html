Ethics · Philosophy of love · Aesthetics · Nietzsche

What makes love a fitting response to a person?

Most of my work starts from that question. We treat some people as worth our time, attention, and care, and others as not — and we take those judgments to be more than arbitrary. I am interested in what could make them correct.

About

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Global and Interdisciplinary Studies at Hosei University in Tokyo, specializing in normative ethics.

My current research concerns the normative dimensions of love: whether love can be merited, what makes it fitting, and how loving relationships bear on a person's well-being. Alongside this, I am co-authoring a book on aesthetic appreciation with Daniel Star. I also work in the history of ethics — chiefly on Nietzsche, and on nineteenth- and twentieth-century British moral theory, especially Henry Sidgwick.

I received my Ph.D. from Boston University in 2023, with a dissertation on reasons for love. Before that I completed an M.A. at Georgia State University and a B.A. in philosophy and classical studies at St. Norbert College. I have taught at Boston University, Boston College, and Northeastern University.

Publications

2024Article

Finite Love

Inquiry, published online 2024.

Published version Preprint
Abstract

Love can be merited, and it is merited by a person's intrinsically valuable qualities. What matters is not that such qualities can be ranked against one another, but that they are gradable: to merit love is to meet a threshold of value. Discerning where those thresholds lie, and who meets them, takes practical wisdom. Since our capacity to love is finite, such judgments belong at the center of philosophizing about love rather than at its margins.

2022Article

Nietzsche on Self-Reverence

The Journal of Nietzsche Studies 53 (2): 181–201.

Published version
Abstract

Respect is ambiguous between a constraint on practical reason and a person-directed pro-attitude, as Darwall has argued. That ambiguity creates trouble for self-respect: in the pro-attitude sense, it lacks warrant. I turn to Nietzsche's notion of self-reverence, which can serve as a replacement for Kantian self-respect in that second sense.

2019Article

Nietzsche, Self-Disgust, and Disgusting Morality

The Journal of Nietzsche Studies 50 (1): 79–105.

Published version
Abstract

Two questions about disgust in Nietzsche: how moral disgust harms, and when it is nonetheless a fitting response. Self-disgust is dangerous because it is characteristic of ascetic morality — but disgust is conditionally fitting insofar as it provokes an immediate adverse reaction to ascetic moral systems, which I call disgusting moralities. I compare Nietzsche's analysis with the Terror Management and Animal-Nature Reminder theories, and argue that his view is compatible with the Entanglement and Co-Opt theses.
